Case study · Immersive Theater · 2022

A MAH-tter of Life and Death

Designing and producing an original immersive murder mystery at the Musée d'Art et d'Histoire de Genève — for 50 guests, 15 cast and crew, in a live museum setting.

Role
Co-creator, experience designer, producer
Context
Fête du Théâtre, commissioned by MAH Geneva
Team
7 main cast, 3 floating, 5 crew — all volunteer
Duration
1h30 live experience, 4 months development

The brief

The Musée d'Art et d'Histoire wanted something new for the 2022 Fête du Théâtre — an experience that would bring a different audience into the museum, particularly young international adults, and help them discover the permanent collection in an unexpected way.

They had run Cluedo-style events since 2013. We proposed something more ambitious: a fully designed immersive theater experience that would transform the Egyptian archaeology wing into a living mystery.

"I want the audience to (re)discover the Musée d'Art et d'Histoire in a fun and entertaining way while discovering a new medium: immersive theater."

The design challenge

The core problem of immersive theater for a general museum audience is participation design: you're working with people who have never done this before, who may be shy, who are in a public space, and who need to feel safe enough to play. At the same time, you need the experience to reward the more adventurous guests without leaving passive participants behind.

We designed a three-phase structure that allowed for multiple levels of engagement simultaneously:

  • Global scenes — the full audience together, following the main narrative. Accessible to everyone, no participation required.
  • Free exploration phases — suspects scattered across rooms, guests could approach, interrogate, and receive missions. Optional but rewarding.
  • Active mechanics — a physical combination lock, voting systems, and side quests for guests who wanted agency.

The setting was late 1800s / early 1900s — an explorer's society, Egyptian artifacts, the codes of Agatha Christie and Indiana Jones. The museum's own collection became part of the story world, not just a backdrop.

Structure and phases

We built the experience in three acts. In Phase 1, guests were divided into two factions on arrival — immediately establishing stakes and social dynamics. A leader announced plans to return a sacred artifact; the crowd was divided. Then: a scream, a body, and an investigation begins.

In Phase 2, suspects dispersed across the museum's rooms. Guests could freely interrogate characters, gather clues, and pursue side missions. A voting mechanism allowed the collective to weigh in on the murder weapon, adding social pressure to the narrative.

Phase 3 returned everyone to a single space for the verdict — followed by a series of flashback scenes reconstructing the crime without revealing the final act. The experience ended with a clear transition ritual to signal the exit from the fiction.

The production reality

This was entirely volunteer — cast, crew, and creative team. That means the challenges weren't just creative. They were organizational, motivational, and political.

Working with an improv troupe means agility and warmth, but it also means different expectations about structure, rehearsal, and creative ownership. Working with a museum means institutional constraints, communication protocols, and objectives that sometimes pull against pure creative ambition.

Managing those tensions — keeping everyone aligned and energized without formal authority or budget — required as much design thinking as the experience itself.

50 guests in the live experience
15 cast and crew members
4mo from proposal to performance

What I learned

The experience confirmed something I've come to believe about immersive design: the participation architecture is the experience. The story, the setting, the costumes — those are in service of a system that decides how people move, choose, and feel agency. Get that wrong and no amount of theatrical quality saves it.

I also learned that the post-mortem and the pitch deck are as important as the show itself. The proposal document I wrote for this project — with its phased structure, interaction levels, audience segmentation, and long-term vision — is what opened the door. That kind of thinking needs to be documented and shared, not just performed once and forgotten.

The "future proposal" section of our original pitch — calling for a more resourced version with sound design, lighting, set design, and ticketed runs — remains something I want to build.

Case study · Theater Forum · 2021–2023

Deviated Lifecourses

Directing a commissioned theater forum on implicit bias for the University of Lausanne — designing the entire methodology, casting, rehearsal process, and performance from scratch, with a cast of non-actor researchers.

Role
Director, writer, facilitator, MC
Client
UNIL Office of Equality & Center LIVES
Format
Theater forum (Theater of the Oppressed)
Performances
2 shows · ~100 total audience · 2022–2023

The brief

The University of Lausanne's Office of Equality and Center LIVES reached out with a clear but difficult ask: illustrate the impact of implicit biases on the lifecourses of researchers — sexism, racism, biases based on physical appearance — for an audience of academics who might themselves be perpetrators without knowing it.

They chose us specifically because we were researchers ourselves. That gave the work credibility and access that a purely theatrical troupe wouldn't have had.

The goals were precise:

  • Show biases the audience recognizes in themselves
  • Show the cumulative impact of those biases on a person's career
  • Show what can actually be done — without providing a false resolution

The methodology challenge

Theater of the Oppressed — specifically forum theater — is a participatory form where scenes depicting oppression are shown, then replayed with audience members stepping in to change the outcome. It requires performers who can improvise, hold character under pressure, and resist "fixing" scenes too easily.

My cast were researchers. Not actors. Selecting them required prioritizing people first: I looked for researchers who were warm and collaborative, had lived experience relevant to the topic, and were comfortable enough with a stage to be trained up. Technical performance skill came second.

The first rehearsal was entirely games and team-building. The cast needed to trust each other before they could be vulnerable in front of each other — let alone an audience.

Writing and rehearsal process

The writing process was genuinely collaborative. Scenes were developed through brainstorming sessions with cast members drawing from their own experiences, then refined through iteration with co-writer Emilie and feedback from the client team.

We ran 3 full rehearsals plus an "asshole workshop" — a dedicated session for cast members to practice committing to being the oppressor character. This sounds uncomfortable because it is. But without it, performers pull their punches, and the scenes don't land with enough reality to provoke genuine audience response.

In addition to group rehearsals, I ran individual calls of 30 minutes to 2 hours with each cast member to check in on their relationship to the material, go through their scenes, and make sure they felt ready.

The performance design

The show used a system of reversed gender and power dynamics — showing oppressive dynamics through a "flipped" lens before revealing the actual situation. This distancing technique allowed the audience to recognize patterns they might otherwise defend.

I MC'ed the full performance: framing each section, managing the transition between scripted scenes and the live forum, and holding the space for audience members who chose to intervene.

The forum section — where audiences come on stage to try different responses — is the hardest part to facilitate. People are reluctant. The room was heavy. I had to balance giving space for genuine response with keeping the event moving and not losing the thread.

2 commissioned performances
~100 total audience members
2023 invited back for HEC/UNIL

What worked and what didn't

The casting approach worked well. The audience responded strongly to scenes precisely because the performers had lived the situations — that authenticity was palpable. Several audience members mentioned recognizing themselves in the oppressor roles, which was the goal.

The forum section was shorter than ideal. With a small audience and limited time, people were warming up to participation just as we had to close. The fix is simple in retrospect: more time budgeted for forum, and audience "plants" to help activate the crowd early.

I also learned to be more explicit with cast about logistics that feel minor but aren't — the show was recorded and streamed without full advance notice to the cast, which made one strong performer uncomfortable enough to step back from that section. That kind of detail belongs in the initial briefing.

What it produced

The client was pleased enough to invite us back for a second run at HEC Lausanne in 2023. The Office of Equality later connected us with the Graduate Institute as a potential future partner. The show is ready to be replayed or adapted — the methodology is documented, the scripts exist, and the framework is replicable.

More than the outputs: this project showed me what I can do when I'm given the trust to design the whole system — not just perform in someone else's. The quality gap between experiences I've designed and experiences I've contributed to is significant, and it comes from that ownership of methodology.
